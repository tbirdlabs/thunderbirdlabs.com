Building Clotho

Everything you need to buy for Clotho and the optional foot pedal, and how to put it all together.

Prices checked October 9, 2026, and they will probably have changed by the time you read this. These are not affiliate links or endorsements, just the parts I used or the closest things I could find on the jungle website.

What it costs

Pretty much everything comes in packs much bigger than one build needs (you need four cup hooks, and the link is for fifty). If you build one by yourself you buy whole packs, which comes to about $185 for Clotho, plus shipping on the motor and driver, and about $17 more for the foot pedal. The parts that actually end up in each Clotho come to about $97, plus about $10 for the pedal, and the rest goes in your parts bin. If you have friends, split the packs. If you don’t, this is a good way to make some.

From your shop

  • PETG for the structural parts, or ABS or ASA if it’s going to live somewhere hot. Clotho takes a bit more than half a roll.
  • A couple feet of 18 AWG stranded wire. A few colors makes things easier, if you have them.
  • About 300 mm (12″) of cotton twine for the brake.
  • A bit of sandpaper, a couple of square inches of 180 grit or so.
  • Optional: a scrap of 1–2 mm neoprene or EPDM for the motor gasket, and some blue Loctite.
  • For the foot pedal: a 1/4″ stereo cable, as long as you like with whatever ends you like, and an M4 × 80 with a nylock nut (or a couple of shorter screws) for the pivot.

Build guide

Most of this should be fairly intuitive once you have the parts in your hands. All the holes and recesses are the right size, and the wrong parts won’t fit or will just look silly. Screw lengths are in the fasteners table above, and the steps call them out where it matters.

Tools

  • A handful of appropriate Allen keys (the screw kits come with them, but nicer ones are…nicer)
  • A pair of needle-nosed pliers
  • A pair of long tweezers (or just use the pliers)
  • A small soft-blow hammer
  • A small screwdriver
  • A soldering iron
  • A heat gun, for joining the belt
  • Optional but nice: a multimeter, to check your wiring before you plug stuff in and let the magic smoke out of the expensive bits
  • Probably a dozen other things I’ve forgotten

Printing

  • PETG for the structural parts, and especially the flyer, which flexes every time it goes around and should bend rather than crack. PLA is actually stiffer, but it’s more brittle and goes soft at a lower temperature. The motor itself shouldn’t get hot unless you’re really hogging it, which you can’t do while actually spinning (unspun wool can’t really take 90 W of linear load), so the heat you need to worry about is wherever it lives. If that’s somewhere hot, or the inside of a car with the windows rolled up, print it in ABS or ASA.
  • 0.20 mm layers, 5 perimeters, 5 top and bottom layers, and an isotropic infill (gyroid or cubic), same as Arachne.
  • All the parts are provided in assemblies; print one of each unless a step tells you otherwise. Supports may be needed in a few places depending on your printer. Unlike Arachne, where I had to choose between printability and function, this time I chose function.
  • The STEP file is the entire CAD model. Your slicer will happily load it and try to print it…but maybe don’t.

Prepare the rod and bearings

  1. Cheap 8 mm rod is usually sheared to length rather than ground, which crushes the last centimeter or so of each end even though it measures a true 8.00 mm in the middle. Wrap the rod in something to protect it, chuck it in a drill, and file a light chamfer (about 0.25 mm) on both ends.
  2. Work the ends down with 180 grit until a 608 slides on and off by hand. The clearance here matters and it’s partly personal preference: go too far and the rod spins in the bearings and makes noise, not far enough and bobbins are a fight to get on and off. The rod should turn the bearing, not spin inside it. Take your time and it can fit and work quite well.
  3. Test-fit every bearing before it goes into a part. If one won’t go on, suspect the rod first.
  4. Cheap 608s tend to come packed with grease thick enough that the rod spins in the inner race instead of the balls rolling. A jar of alcohol and a shot of silicone spray takes care of that.

Risers, base and electronics

  1. Press the two halves of the front riser together by hand to check the alignment, then gently tap them into place. Do the same for the rear riser, but put a tested 608 in its cavity first.
  2. Bolt each riser’s halves together with three M4×10 and hex nuts. Longer bolts will hit the flyer, and shorter ones may not bite enough thread.
  3. Optional: a 1–2 mm neoprene or EPDM gasket between the motor face and the front riser quiets things down a bit by breaking the path the noise takes into the frame. Not required, just nice. If you want it, now’s the time.
  4. Attach the motor to the front riser with four M5×20, flat washers in front and lock washers and nuts in back. Clearance around the motor flange is tight, about 2.5 mm, so get creative with your tweezers if you need to. I had to stuff a large screwdriver between the nuts and the motor to hold them while I tightened the bolts.
  5. Fit the motor brackets to the motor with two M3×12 each.
  6. Install the pulley on the motor’s D-shaft and tighten the grub screws.
  7. Wire up the switch, speed pot and power jack following the wiring diagram (v10; it’s also in the files). Cut the wires long for now; we’ll trim them later. Folding the pins over before soldering the wires on can buy a bit of extra clearance.
    • The motor’s phase wires and hall sensor wires are both yellow, green and blue, and the only way to tell them apart is the gauge: the phase wires are the fat ones (20 AWG) and the hall wires are the skinny ones (26 AWG). This is the easiest wiring mistake to make.
    • The BLD-510B has shipped with its enable logic inverted between revisions (the manual itself shows two versions behaving opposite ways), and the revision printed on the board won’t necessarily tell you which one you have. Bench-test yours.
  8. Before you power anything up, turn both trim pots (speed limit and current limit) all the way down. The driver ships set to 3,500 RPM and full current, which is more than enough to put the flyer into low earth orbit.
  9. With everything still on the bench, hook it all up to the driver and test that the motor spins, reverses, and has speed control. Really, do this step. Once it’s assembled most of the wiring is hidden, and I promise you won’t want to take the thing apart to find a fault.
  10. Disconnect everything from the driver and install the jack, pot and switch in the risers and electronics housing.
  11. Route the pot and switch wiring through the electronics housing, push an M4 square nut into the recess in the back of it, and bolt it to the front riser with an M4×20. Put the speed knob on the pot.
  12. At the same time, with your third and fourth hands, push square nuts into the front riser slot in the base, set the motor, front riser and electronics housing in place, and bolt them to the base from underneath with two M4×16. If a square nut won’t stay put, run a screw into it temporarily.
  13. Bolt each motor bracket to the base with an M4×12 and a square nut.
  14. Before the next step, screw the brake tension screw into its spreader and work it in and out a few times. Depending on your tolerances it can be very stiff at first, but it wears in quickly.
  15. Bolt both spreaders and the electronics housing to the base from underneath with M4×12 and square nuts (M4×20s work here too, if that’s what you have).
  16. Slide the driver into its recess in the spreaders, heatsink up so the flyer cools it, and bolt it in with an M4×20 and a nut through the electronics housing and two M3×20 and nuts through the brake spring retainer. Route all the wiring underneath, trim it to length, and connect it. Don’t forget the wiring from the rear riser.
  17. Set square nuts in the rear riser and in the rear of the spreaders (making sure to put one in the oddly tilted rear-facing slot), then bolt the rear riser to the base and spreaders with six M4×16. Route everything carefully, and remember the brake tension screw is going right where that rat’s nest is now.
  18. Test everything again: switch centered (off), knob at minimum, power on. The green run light should be steady and the red alarm light off. Flip the switch one way and make sure the motor spins smoothly in that direction at low speed, then stop it and check the other way. Always stop the motor before reversing.

Flyer and bobbin

  1. With the flyer body on a smooth, hard surface, gently tap the rod into its recess. It may take a bit of force, but you should hear the sound change when the rod bottoms out. Or, if you left it chucked up in the drill from the rod prep, just spin it in.
  2. You can lightly lubricate the rod at this point if you like. Use something dry, like silicone or white lithium, and wipe off all the excess.
  3. Put two M4 square nuts into each flyer arm and attach the arms to the flyer body with M4×12 in the inside holes (toward the orifice) and M4×20 in the outside holes.
  4. Onto the threaded end of the orifice put a bearing spacer, the 6804 bearing, and the second bearing spacer, in that order. Screw the orifice into the flyer body.
  5. Test-fit the flyer hooks on the arms. They should slide smoothly with very little friction; if they catch, lightly sand the arms with 180 grit until they don’t. Sand them under running water to keep the dust down, or rinse them afterwards to get rid of the powder and bring back their factory sheen.
  6. The flyer takes four hooks, two on each arm, and the flyer assembly file only has two, so print another pair (or just copy the hook in your slicer). With only one per arm the flyer goes out of balance as soon as the hooks aren’t in matching spots, and it gets worse the faster you spin. Thread a cup hook into the small hole in each flyer hook, press an M4 square nut into each one, and start an M4×12 thumbscrew, keeping the tip out of the arm slot. Slide the hooks onto the arms and secure them with the thumbscrews. It takes very little force; don’t overtighten them.
  7. For the bobbin, press a 608 into the front flange and screw the rear flange into it. Press the bearing spacer into the rear flange, press the second 608 in after it, and screw on the rear bearing retainer. Arachne bobbins work on Clotho too.

Final assembly

  1. Tie about 300 mm (12″) of cotton twine to the brake tension screw’s barrel and screw it into its spreader. It should be snug but turnable with your fingertips: too loose and it won’t hold tension, too tight and it’s a pain to use. If it’s too loose, push a 10 mm piece of the belt into the slot in the threads.
  2. Hook the brake spring into the brake spring retainer with a thumbscrew through its eye and into the square nut you put there earlier, so it doesn’t fall off every time you let off the tension. Tie the twine to the spring.
  3. Set the flyer and bobbin in place: tilt the assembly, push the rod into the 608 in the rear riser, then lower the front bearing into its saddle on the front riser. Removal is the reverse. If you lift too much before pulling it out the bearing binds, if you push too far the rod binds, and if you miss the bearing nothing goes together. It may be awkward the first couple of times, but everyone who has played with it catches on quickly.
  4. Route the brake line over the bobbin’s brake pulley.
  5. Cut about 200 mm of belt and join the ends with your preferred method. I use a heat gun and a small jig, which is included in the print files.
  6. Put the belt on. In use, the belt is what holds the front bearing in its saddle and the brake line holds the rear bearing in place.

Setup

  1. Switch centered (off), apply power. Green run light steady, red alarm light off. If the red light is on, stop and check your wiring before going any further.
  2. The two trim pots on the driver (speed limit and current limit) are small unmarked crosses. Both should still be all the way counterclockwise.
  3. Set the current limit to about 50% first.
  4. With the flyer and belt on and the speed knob at maximum, slowly and carefully bring up the speed limit pot to the fastest speed you think you’ll ever want. Don’t get crazy; it’s nicer to have a usable, easily selectable top speed than a terrifying one, and it’s easy to turn up later. A meter that reads Hz on the driver’s speed output will give you the exact number if you care, but you don’t need it.
  5. Then do your spinning with the knob or the foot pedal below that. At the very bottom of the knob’s travel there’s a dead stop where the driver stops the motor.
  6. Turn the knob down, reverse, and bring it back up to check the other direction. If S and Z come out backwards, pop the switch out and flip it over; the mount is symmetrical for exactly that reason.

Changing bobbins

Take the belt off, lift the front of the flyer out of its saddle, and pull the rod out of the rear bearing (the bearing pivots in its cavity to let it come out at an angle). Putting it back is the reverse. No tools, and no loose parts to lose in the couch.

Foot pedal (optional)

This is optional, but fun. The assembly is simple enough that it barely needs instructions.

  1. Print the pedal parts and assemble the pivot. Either one M4×80 (or a 5/32″ × 3″ machine screw) with a nylock nut or a couple of shorter screws will work, but the shorter ones will probably make the tension easier to adjust.
  2. Wire the pot, the Pro Micro and the jacks following the wiring diagram, and flash the firmware from the files. Then calibrate it and set the dead zones on the pedal setup page, or by hand with the serial commands in the comments at the top of the source code.
  3. The pedal only swings the pot through about a fifth of its rotation, which is why there’s a microcontroller in there at all: it calibrates itself to your pedal. Plugging the pedal in disconnects the knob on the spinner.
